Reward-based foundations

Build skills, one small win at a time.

Choose a practical skill for home, walks, or everyday confidence. Every lesson shows what to prepare, what to watch for, and when to make it easier.

Let your dog choose, reward the behavior you want, and pause if they look worried or stop engaging.
✦

Keep it kind and easy. Reward a choice you like, pause before your dog gets tired, and change one challenge at a time.

01 · Everyday cuesSkills for everyday life5 lessons
01

Name & attention

Teach your dog that their name predicts something good and an easy chance to reconnect.

Goal: One name cue, then a willing glance or head turn.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Start close by in a quiet room, with a few small rewards ready.
  2. Say the name once in a warm voice. The instant your dog turns toward you, mark with “yes.”
  3. Give a reward, then let them look away and reset. Repeat a few easy times.
  4. Practice in a new room only after the first one feels easy.

If it’s hard: Move closer, reduce distractions, or try a more exciting reward. Don’t repeat the name louder.

02

Sit

A useful pause for greetings and daily routines. Let your dog find the position without being pushed into it.

Goal: A relaxed sit they can leave when released.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Hold a treat near the nose, then move it slowly up and slightly back.
  2. As your dog’s bottom touches the floor, mark “yes” and reward.
  3. After a few easy repetitions, say “sit” just before the movement you already see.
  4. Use a release word such as “all done,” then invite another try.

If it’s hard: Reward a smaller head lift or try on a non-slip surface. Never press down on the dog’s back.

03

Down

Teach a comfortable lie-down as a separate cue from a relaxed, self-chosen settle.

Goal: Front elbows lower to a soft surface by choice.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. With your dog standing or sitting, move a treat from the nose down between the front paws.
  2. Mark and reward a comfortable elbow bend or full down. Build in small steps.
  3. When the movement is easy, say “down” once just before your hand signal.
  4. Reward, then release so your dog knows the position is not endless.

If it’s hard: Try a rug or bed, reward small progress, and stop if lowering looks painful. Don’t pull legs or push shoulders.

04

Stay & wait

Build a brief pause with a clear release. Add time, distance, and distractions separately.

Goal: A short, calm pause—not a test of endurance.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Ask for an easy sit or down, say “stay,” and pause for one second.
  2. Reward while you are still close, then say your release word.
  3. Gradually add a second or a small step away. Return to reward before your dog gets up.
  4. Change one challenge at a time. Practice behind a gate or on leash around doors.

If it’s hard: Shorten the pause or distance. A stay never replaces a barrier or leash near traffic.

05

Come when called

Make coming back worthwhile. Recall should not predict that every fun thing is over.

Goal: Turn toward you and return in a safe, easy setting.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Begin a few steps away indoors. Say your recall cue once in a happy voice.
  2. Move back a little. When your dog reaches you, mark and give a generous reward.
  3. Release them to explore again when it is safe; this keeps coming back from ending all the fun.
  4. Practice outdoors only in a secure area or on a well-attached long line.

If it’s hard: Get closer and lower the distraction. Don’t call when you cannot keep your dog safe or reward the return.

02 · Everyday lifeWalks, trades & calm care5 lessons
06

Loose-leash walking

Teach that staying near you keeps the walk moving, with plenty of safe sniff breaks.

Goal: A soft curve in the leash, not a perfect heel.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Start somewhere quiet with a comfortable, well-fitted harness and leash.
  2. Mark and reward when the leash is slack or your dog checks in beside you.
  3. If the leash tightens, stop or gently change direction. Resume when it softens; never jerk the leash.
  4. Practice near harder distractions only at a distance where your dog can still respond.

If it’s hard: Choose a quieter route or add distance. Sniffing and exploring are part of a good walk.

07

Leave it

Teach your dog to turn away from an item before they pick it up. It is not a substitute for safe management.

Goal: Disengage from a safe practice item and check back.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Place a low-value treat in a closed hand. Let your dog sniff without saying anything.
  2. When they move their nose away, mark “yes” and reward from your other hand.
  3. Repeat until your dog can turn away easily, then add the cue “leave it.”
  4. Only later practice with safe items on the floor, covered by your hand or foot at first.

Safety: Keep medicines, food hazards, and rubbish out of reach. Use a barrier and a trade for dangerous finds.

08

Drop it

Teach a calm release of something already in your dog’s mouth—using a fair trade, never force.

Goal: Release a safe toy and feel good about the exchange.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Offer a toy that is safe to hold and a more valuable treat.
  2. Hold the treat near the nose. When your dog lets go, mark “yes” and give the treat.
  3. When your dog is relaxed about trading, add “drop it” just before offering the treat.
  4. Whenever safe, give the toy back so releasing does not always end the game.

Safety: Don’t pry the mouth open or chase. For guarding, use distance and get a qualified professional’s plan.

09

Settle on a mat

Help your dog discover a comfortable resting place while everyday life carries on.

Goal: Soft muscles and voluntary rest—not a forced down-stay.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Put a mat in a quiet spot and reward your dog for looking at or stepping onto it.
  2. Drop a treat between the front paws. Reward easy choices to stay, sit, or lie down.
  3. Place another treat on the mat before your dog needs to leave. Use a release cue.
  4. Practice while calm before adding mild household activity.

If it’s hard: Make the room quieter or let your dog choose another safe resting place. Never tether them to the mat.

10

Cooperative handling

Make paw, ear, brushing, harness, and vet-care practice predictable and easy to pause.

Goal: A dog who can opt in and signal they need a break.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Choose one touch your dog already tolerates, such as briefly touching the shoulder.
  2. Touch for one second, then offer a treat. Stop and give your dog room.
  3. Repeat only while the body stays loose and the dog chooses to return.
  4. Build toward a paw, ear, brush, or harness in tiny steps across separate sessions.

Stop and reset: If your dog freezes, pulls away, growls, or hides, stop. Don’t use practice to push through pain or fear.

03 · A good home startRoutines & new experiences2 lessons
11

Toilet-training routine

Prevent accidents by planning frequent chances to go outside and rewarding success right where it happens.

Goal: A predictable routine and a safe, easy-to-reach toilet spot.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Take your dog to the same safe spot after waking, meals, play, and before rest; learn their individual signs.
  2. Stay nearby and keep the moment calm. As soon as they finish, praise gently and reward outside.
  3. At home, supervise or use a safe pen so your dog is not left to guess where to go.
  4. If there is an accident, clean the spot thoroughly and adjust the routine—no scolding.

Get help: New or frequent accidents after a reliable routine can have a medical cause; ask your veterinarian.

12

Positive socialization

Help a young dog feel safe around ordinary people, places, sounds, surfaces, and handling—without making them greet everyone.

Goal: Calm curiosity, choice, and the ability to move away.

Start lesson
Quick steps
  1. Choose one new sight or sound at a distance where your puppy can still sniff, eat, or look away.
  2. Pair a brief look with a treat or play, then let the puppy pause or leave.
  3. Keep it short and pleasant. Repeat another day instead of moving closer right away.
  4. Ask your veterinarian about local infection risks and safer places to explore before the vaccine series is complete.

Slow down: If your puppy freezes, hides, tries to escape, or stops taking food, add distance and end the exposure. Never force a greeting.
